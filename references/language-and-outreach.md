# Language and Outreach

## When to load

Load this file for two related but distinct tasks:

1. **Academic reasoning-language refinement**: formal academic phrasing, hedging, significance language, sentence-level logic, paragraph transitions, contribution language, conclusion language, or revision of prose that already belongs to a research document.
2. **Scientific correspondence / research outreach**: concise emails to professors, PIs, supervisors, collaborators, advisors, labs, or research-position contacts.

This file covers P001/P009 for cross-paper academic reasoning-language and P010 for research outreach. It is not a generic grammar editor, synonym generator, manuscript template, proposal template, or reviewer-response architecture.

## Boundary gate before rewriting

Language refinement cannot rescue weak research logic. Before rewriting, diagnose whether the problem is actually upstream:

| If the weakness is... | Do this before polishing |
|---|---|
| Vague gap, novelty, problem, RQ, aim, or objective | Load `references/cluster-gap-problem.md`. |
| Author-by-author summary, missing synthesis, weak field positioning | Load `references/cluster-literature-review.md`. |
| Proposal/front-end rationale, aims, significance, or study setup is incoherent | Load `references/cluster-front-end-proposal.md`. |
| Manuscript sections do not align with aim, Methods, Results, or claims | Load `references/manuscript-and-results.md`. |
| Discussion, limitation, implication, conclusion, or unexpected-result logic is weak | Load `references/discussion-conclusion.md`. |
| Reviewer response is defensive, vague, or missing evidence/location of changes | Load `references/peer-review-revision.md`. |

If the user asks only to “make this sound more academic,” first identify whether the sentence has a defensible claim, evidence base, scope, and reasoning move. If not, explain the weakness briefly and offer a stronger reasoning structure before a polished version.

For outreach, recipient-specific claims require supplied or retrieved evidence. If the user has not provided the professor/lab page, paper, project, current opening, or research theme, produce a fit-audit checklist or email with explicit placeholders rather than inventing fit.

## Reasoning-language vs generic polish

Generic polish fixes grammar, fluency, diction, and surface tone. **Reasoning-language refinement** makes the intellectual move clear: what the sentence is doing, how it relates to evidence, how strong the claim is, and how it moves the argument forward.

Use P001/P009 as reasoning anchors, not decorative phrase banks. Do not mechanically insert “however,” “it is evident,” or “this underscores” into unsupported prose. A phrase is useful only when it clarifies a real move.

| Reasoning move | What to diagnose | Runtime repair |
|---|---|---|
| Contrast | Is the opposition real or merely a change of topic? | Make the basis of difference explicit: method, population, finding, theory, context, or assumption. |
| Extension | Does the sentence add new substance or repeat? | Show how the next point broadens, deepens, specifies, or applies the prior point. |
| Synthesis | Are multiple sources being integrated rather than listed? | Name the pattern, convergence, divergence, tension, or field-level implication. |
| Definition | Is the key term scoped for this study? | Define the term in context and avoid universal definitions unless supported. |
| Evidence | Is the claim anchored in data, studies, or observations? | Convert personal opinion into evidence-backed or source-limited language. |
| Justification | Does the reader know why the step matters? | Link the claim to gap, aim, method choice, interpretation, or contribution. |
| Hedging | Is the claim too strong for the evidence? | Add calibrated caution without making the argument timid or vague. |
| Limitation | Is the boundary specific and meaningful? | State what the evidence cannot show and why that matters. |
| Significance | Is importance asserted or demonstrated? | Tie significance to theoretical, empirical, methodological, practical, or population-level stakes. |
| Contribution | Is the contribution distinct from topic description? | State what the study adds, clarifies, tests, extends, resolves, or enables. |
| Transition | Does the next paragraph follow logically? | Signal the relation: consequence, contrast, gap, method move, implication, or synthesis. |
| Conclusion | Does the closing compress the argument? | Synthesize the point without repeating every result or overclaiming. |

## Runtime procedure for academic language work

1. **Label the intended move**: contrast, evidence, significance, limitation, contribution, transition, conclusion, or another move.
2. **Check the claim**: identify subject, evidence, scope, strength, and relationship to the document’s aim or section purpose.
3. **Repair logic before style**: if the claim is vague, unsupported, or misplaced, give a compact diagnosis and hand off to the relevant task reference.
4. **Rewrite precisely**: preserve the user’s meaning; improve hierarchy, connection, caution, and clarity.
5. **Avoid ornamental inflation**: do not make simple claims sound grander than the evidence permits.
6. **Return a usable output**: provide the revised sentence/paragraph plus a short note naming the reasoning move changed.

Default language output pattern:

- **Move diagnosed:** the reasoning move being attempted.
- **Logic status:** ready to polish, needs scope/evidence, or needs upstream repair.
- **Revision:** the improved sentence/paragraph if enough context exists.
- **Why stronger:** one concise explanation tied to evidence, scope, flow, or tone.

If logic is weak, replace the revision with: **logic problem -> stronger structure -> provisional wording -> evidence needed**.

Use formal manuscript/proposal language when the audience expects dense, evidence-grounded argument. Use concise person-to-person language for outreach.

## P010 outreach structure

Research outreach must be specific, fit-based, concise, and direct. It should answer: who is writing, why this recipient, what evidence proves fit, what the sender can contribute, and what specific ask is being made.

Use this structure for cold or semi-warm research emails:

| Email part | Purpose | Runtime guidance |
|---|---|---|
| Subject | Make the ask and topic legible | Keep it short: prospective student, research alignment, collaboration query, RA inquiry, meeting request. |
| Sender identity | Establish who the sender is | One sentence: degree/stage, institution/role, field, and immediate context. |
| Recipient-specific fit | Prove the email is not generic | Mention a specific paper, project, finding, method, dataset, theory, or lab theme and why it aligns. |
| Evidence of fit | Show relevant preparation | Name prior project, technique, dataset, thesis, publication, analysis, fieldwork, or skill; avoid unsupported self-praise. |
| Specific contribution | Show what the sender brings | Connect the sender’s skill/background to a concrete direction in the recipient’s lab or agenda. |
| Direct ask | Tell the reader what to do next | Ask about openings, supervision, collaboration, a brief meeting, feedback, or whether they are accepting students. |
| Concise close | Make response easy | Thank them, offer flexibility, and avoid long emotional biography. |
| Attachment logic | Add credibility when appropriate | Mention CV attached when seeking a position, supervision, lab placement, or research role; add writing sample/proposal only if relevant or requested. |

Do not write outreach like a manuscript abstract. Do not write a full proposal inside the email. If research fit is underdeveloped, produce a fit audit or missing-information checklist rather than a bloated template.

## Formal style vs outreach style

| Dimension | Manuscript/proposal style | Outreach style |
|---|---|---|
| Primary goal | Develop an argument for a scholarly reader | Earn a reply from a specific person |
| Evidence | Citations, data, conceptual logic | Specific paper/project fit plus sender track record |
| Sentence density | Can be complex if controlled | Short, direct, and readable |
| Tone | Formal, cautious, structured | Professional, warm, specific, not deferentially inflated |
| Length | Section-dependent | Usually compact; every sentence should show fit or ask |
| Failure mode | Overclaiming, weak synthesis, decorative formality | Generic flattery, vague “I love your work,” life story, indirect ask |

## Output selector

Choose the smallest useful output:

| User asks for... | Produce... |
|---|---|
| “What is wrong with this wording?” | Reasoning-move diagnosis with a repair priority. |
| “Rewrite this sentence/paragraph” | Precise rewrite plus one-line explanation of the changed move. |
| “Make it less strong / more careful” | Hedging and tone revision calibrated to evidence. |
| “Make the significance clearer” | Significance-language repair tied to stakes, gap, or contribution. |
| “Does this email fit the professor?” | Fit audit: recipient-specific evidence, sender evidence, contribution, ask. |
| “Write a cold email” | Concise outreach email with placeholders only where information is missing. |
| “Improve my ask” | Direct-ask refinement with one or two sharper options. |

## Non-negotiables

- Do not provide exhaustive phrase banks unless the user explicitly asks and the reasoning move is clear.
- Do not replace words mechanically with “academic” synonyms.
- Do not use phrases to decorate weak logic.
- Do not treat P010 as manuscript, proposal, literature-review, or peer-review architecture.
- Do not fabricate source grounding, lab fit, recipient details, citations, skills, publications, or methodological credibility.
- Do not imply current openings, current lab priorities, or recent papers unless supplied by the user or retrieved from current sources.
