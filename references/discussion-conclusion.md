# Discussion and Conclusion

## When to load

Load this file for Discussion sections or chapters, interpretation of findings, implications, limitations, future work, unexpected or contradictory results, closing synthesis, and Conclusions. Use it when the user has findings and needs to move from “what happened” toward “what it means,” or when a conclusion needs to compress a completed argument.

Do not load it as the primary resource for Results reporting. Use `references/manuscript-and-results.md` for Results discipline and P006 local findings language, then return here only when interpretation, implications, limitations, or closing synthesis are needed.

## Results vs Discussion vs Conclusion

| Section | Main job | Typical content | Boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Results | Report findings that answer aims/objectives. | Associations, differences, patterns, trends, null findings, table/figure anchors. | Avoid literature explanation, mechanisms, implications, and recommendations unless the genre combines sections. |
| Discussion | Interpret findings and connect them to literature, theory, practice, limitations, and future work. | Meaning, significance, comparison with prior work, unexpected results, implications, limitations, specific future directions. | Do not repeat detailed Results or introduce new results. |
| Conclusion | Compress the completed argument into a final, scoped synthesis. | Purpose, key finding(s), contribution, limitation/scope, implication/recommendation, final value. | Do not re-run the full Discussion or overgeneralize beyond the design/data. |

## Discussion workflow

Use this sequence after identifying the user’s genre, audience, and available findings. Keep the Discussion proportional to the strength of the data.

1. **Purpose anchor.** Briefly remind the reader what the study set out to investigate. Do not rebuild the Introduction.
2. **Key findings, not full Results.** Select the findings that matter for the aim, argument, or contribution. Avoid numbers unless needed for meaning.
3. **Interpret each key finding.** Ask: What does this finding mean in relation to the research question? What does it suggest, refine, challenge, or complicate?
4. **Connect to literature/theory/practice.** Compare with prior work through agreement, contradiction, extension, boundary condition, or mechanism. If the literature synthesis is missing, hand off to `references/cluster-literature-review.md`.
5. **Explain unexpected or contradictory results.** Treat surprise as an intellectual opportunity, not automatically an error. Use P019 routes below.
6. **Draw implications.** Separate theoretical, practical, policy, clinical, educational, organizational, or methodological implications as appropriate. Be specific about who can use the insight and how.
7. **Acknowledge limitations.** Name limitations that affect interpretation, transferability, measurement, internal validity, external validity, sampling, design, or scope. Do not apologize generically.
8. **Link limitations to interpretation.** Explain what a limitation does and does not undermine. A limitation without consequence is decorative.
9. **Specify future work.** Derive future studies from findings, limitations, contradictions, new questions, or under-tested mechanisms. Avoid “future research is needed” without naming what, why, and under what design/context.
10. **End with synthesis.** State the larger meaning of the Discussion without repeating every finding.

### Discussion architecture table

| Move | Question to answer | Drafting cue | Failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|
| Key finding | What matters most? | “The study found that...” | Full Results repetition |
| Interpretation | What does it mean? | “This suggests/may indicate...” | Description only |
| Literature/theory | How does it relate to the field? | “This extends/challenges/aligns with...” | Unsupported claim or citation dump |
| Implication | So what, and for whom? | “For X, this means...” | Generic importance language |
| Limitation | What constrains the claim? | “Because..., the findings should be interpreted as...” | Token limitation list |
| Future work | What precise next study follows? | “Future studies should test/examine...” | Vague “more research” sentence |
| Synthesis | What is the final meaning? | “Together, these findings...” | Conclusion-style overclaim |

## Unexpected-results reasoning

When the user asks why a result was unexpected, first decide whether they are writing Results or Discussion. In Results, name the unexpected pattern without explaining it. In Discussion, develop plausible explanations.

Use these routes:

| Explanation route | Diagnostic prompt | Safe wording logic |
|---|---|---|
| Design | Did study design, timing, intervention dose, comparison group, or analytic choice shape the result? | “One possible explanation is that the design captured...” |
| Measurement | Could instruments, operational definitions, timing of measurement, or scale sensitivity affect the pattern? | “The finding may reflect how X was measured...” |
| Sample | Could age, experience, clinical profile, socioeconomic profile, subgroup composition, recruitment, or sample size matter? | “Sample characteristics may have contributed to...” |
| Context | Did cultural, institutional, temporal, policy, environmental, or implementation conditions differ from prior work? | “In this setting, contextual factors may have...” |
| Literature | Are there minority, contradictory, newer, or field-specific studies that align with the finding? | “Although unexpected, this result is consistent with...” |
| Theory/mechanism | Does the finding suggest a different pathway, moderator, threshold, or boundary condition? | “This pattern may indicate that the expected relationship depends on...” |

Boundary: do not treat unexpected results as errors by default. Also do not explain them with unsupported speculation. Mark possibilities as plausible, tentative, or requiring further testing.

## Limitation and implication audit

Use this quick audit before drafting or revising:

- Does each limitation name the affected design/data feature?
- Does it explain how interpretation is constrained?
- Does it avoid invalidating the entire study unless the flaw truly does?
- Does each implication follow from a finding, not from the author’s preference?
- Is the implication scoped to population, setting, design, sample, or data strength?
- Does each future-work recommendation follow from a finding, limitation, contradiction, or mechanism?

Repair weak limitation language by moving from label to consequence: “The small sample size was a limitation” -> “Because the sample was small and drawn from one setting, the findings should be interpreted as preliminary evidence of X rather than as an estimate generalizable to Y.”

## Conclusion workflow

Use P008 when the user needs a conclusion, final paragraph, chapter close, report close, or compressed study takeaway. Conclusions should feel complete but not inflated.

Build the conclusion in this order:

1. **Purpose restatement.** One sentence that re-anchors the study or argument.
2. **Key findings only.** Select the most important result(s), pattern(s), or answer(s). Do not repeat detailed statistics.
3. **Direct answer/contribution.** State what the study adds to knowledge, practice, theory, method, or local understanding.
4. **Scope/limitation.** Briefly name the main boundary that affects interpretation if needed.
5. **Implication or recommendation.** State the most defensible implication or next action.
6. **Closing value.** End with a forward-looking or field-facing statement that stays inside the evidence.

Conclusion compression test: if the paragraph needs multiple citations, several mechanism explanations, or detailed limitations, it is probably still Discussion material.
